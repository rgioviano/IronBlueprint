# Email Copywriting Research Brief, applied to The Achieve Minute

Prepared for Ryne Gioviano, Achieve Sports Performance, Aurora IL. Date: October 14, 2026.
Scope: subject lines, preview text, opening hooks, structure, length, story vs tips, CTA and P.S., plain text vs designed, voice for parents vs athletes, and driving replies. Every number below names its source. Anything that could not be verified is marked **UNVERIFIED** and is not used as a rule.

---

## Part 1. What the sources say

### 1.1 Measurement first: open rates are noisy
- Mailchimp benchmarks (last updated December 2023, campaigns to 1,000 or more subscribers): all-user average open rate 35.63%, click rate 2.62%, unsubscribe rate 0.22%. Health and fitness: 37.07% opens, 1.87% clicks. Source: Mailchimp, https://mailchimp.com/resources/email-marketing-benchmarks/ and the Mailchimp benchmark PDF linked from that page.
- A "Sports" category at 41.84% opens and 2.88% clicks is attributed to Mailchimp by a secondary site (wethrift.com). I did not see it on the Mailchimp page itself, so treat it as **UNVERIFIED** at the primary source.
- Litmus Email Client Market Share (2026 page): Apple Mail, including Mail Privacy Protection (MPP), shows 62.26% of tracked opens, and Litmus states MPP affects roughly 55 to 60% of all opens. Apple and Gmail together are close to 90% of opens. Source: https://www.litmus.com/email-client-market-share/
- Litmus explains that MPP pre-fetches email content, so Apple Mail users can look like they opened when they did not. Source: https://www.litmus.com/blog/identifying-real-opens-mail-privacy-protection
- **What this means:** do not judge an issue by its open rate. Judge it by replies, clicks, and booked evaluations.

### 1.2 Plain text vs designed
- HubSpot A/B tests: "In every single A/B test, the simpler-designed email won," with statistical significance. Plain text vs a GIF version: plain text got 42% more clicks. Plain text vs an HTML template with images: the HTML version got 51% fewer clicks. A simple HTML template vs a heavy one: the simple one got 30% more clicks. Source: HubSpot, https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/plain-text-vs-html-emails-data
- HubSpot, Science of Email Marketing (2014): nearly two thirds of surveyed professionals said they *preferred* image-based or HTML email, yet in observed data click rate fell as the number of images went up, with close to a one-point drop from zero images to one. In the same report, emails with 300 to 500 characters of body text had the highest click rates in that dataset. Source: https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hub/53/file-2191769206-pdf/Science_of_Email_Marketing_2014.pdf
- Caveat: these are HubSpot's lists and B2B-leaning audiences, and the 300 to 500 character finding is about clicks on a link, not replies or reading. It is a direction, not a law.
- Gary Halbert, *The Boron Letters* (local copy, /workspace/books/classic-sales-marketing/Boron-Letters-Halbert.md): people sort mail into an "A-pile" of letters that look personal and a "B-pile" of things that look like advertising. The A-pile gets read. This is the old-mail version of the HubSpot finding.
- **What this means:** the current format (white background, Helvetica, no heavy template) is right. Keep it looking like a note from Ryne, with one image at most.

### 1.3 Subject lines and preview text
- Halbert (Boron Letters, local copy): the "attention grabber" must be relevant and tie to the message. Attention that does not connect to the body wastes the reader.
- Local file copywriter-master-copy-insights.md (Hopkins, Halbert, Shaw notes): treat the subject line as a headline, say plainly who it is for and what they get, call out the exact reader, and avoid clever "blind" lines that draw the wrong readers. Curiosity is fine only if the first body line pays it off.
- Local file copywriter-new-books-insights.md (Collier, Haldeman-Julius, Shaw notes): proven title forms are "How to...", "Facts you should know about...", and "What X should mean to you."
- Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966): match the message to the reader's state of awareness (unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware). A parent list that already knows the gym is mostly product aware, so the subject line can name the topic directly instead of hiding it.
- Specific numeric claims often repeated online (for example "subject lines under X characters get Y% more opens", or "preview text lifts opens by Z%") could not be tied to a primary source in this pass. **UNVERIFIED**, not used.
- Preview text: Mailchimp lets you set it (the "preview text" field in the campaign builder). If it is left blank, most inboxes show the first line of the body, which for us is "Hi Name," and wastes the space. That behavior is standard, but I have no verified lift figure.
- MPP (Litmus, above) makes open-rate subject line tests unreliable for Apple users. If testing subject lines, judge on clicks or replies.

### 1.4 Opening hooks
- Local master-copy file (Atkinson): the first line after the headline decides whether they keep reading. Sequence is attention, first impression, curiosity, interest.
- Local master-copy file (Hopkins, Letter Writing): continue the line that created the interest. The first sentence should pick up exactly where the subject line left off.
- Hormozi notes (local /workspace/hormozi-advice-videos/COPY.md): get to the meat on the first screen, and lead with proof so the promise is believable.

### 1.5 Structure and length
- Robert Collier (local new-books file): a letter has six parts: an opening that fits the reader's train of thought, a picture, the reason why, proof, a reason to act now, and a close that says exactly what to do. Skimmers read the first paragraph and the last, so the last one must carry the ask.
- Shaw (local files): write for three readers: the one who reads subject plus last line, the skimmer, and the word-by-word reader. Each should get the point.
- HubSpot 2014 (above): 300 to 500 characters of text correlated with the best click rate in its data. For a teaching newsletter the job is reading and replying, not one click, so a short tip of roughly 150 to 250 words is a reasonable house target. That word range is a judgment call, not a sourced figure.

### 1.6 Story vs tips
- Ben Settle (daily email practitioner, author of the *Email Players* newsletter): argues for "infotainment," meaning useful content wrapped in personality or story, sent consistently, so readers look forward to it. This is his practitioner view, not a measured study.
- Hormozi notes (local COPY.md): stories that changed behavior beat "6 tips." State the facts and emphasize, do not manufacture.
- Local master-copy file: story emails use real stories only, with permission.
- **What this means:** one tip per issue, opened with a short real moment from the gym when one exists. No invented athletes.

### 1.7 CTA and P.S.
- Local master-copy file (Halbert): the P.S. restates the next step; it is not a second, different pitch.
- Collier (local): skimmers jump to the end, so the end carries the ask. The P.S. is the most-read spot near the end of a letter. This is copywriting tradition; I found no current primary study with a P.S. readership number, so any percentage you see quoted is **UNVERIFIED**.
- Hopkins via local file: give a true reason to act now (a season date, a block start). Never "limited time" with no reason.
- One ask per email. Maya and Ryne already set this: the closing line signs off and the P.S. is the only ask.

### 1.8 Driving replies
- Local files (Shaw, master copy): script the reply. Tell them exactly what to send, such as one word or "grade, sport, and two after-school times," and remove the last excuse if it is true.
- Local master-copy file: make the action physical and small ("Reply YES").
- Replies also help: a reply is a real engagement signal that MPP cannot fake. That is a reasoned point, not a sourced figure.

### 1.9 Voice for parents vs athletes
- Schwartz awareness levels (above) and Halbert's call-out rule: write to one reader. For this list that reader is the parent.
- Parent wants: is this safe, is it worth the time, will it help my kid's skill and confidence. Athlete wants: what do I do, and how will I feel it in the water or on the field. Write the tip to the parent and include one line the parent can hand to the athlete ("Tell your swimmer: ...").
- Hormozi notes: truth over costume. No hype words.

## Part 2. Applied to The Achieve Minute

### 2.1 House rules (locked)
- Mailchimp, white background, Helvetica body.
- Greeting: Hi *|FNAME|*,
- One tip per issue. Mostly for parents, athletes second.
- No prices. No dashes used as long pauses; use periods, commas, and parentheses.
- Call the intake a "20-minute screen" or an "evaluation." Never use the banned intake words or the banned hype words in the brand lock.
- Youth copy covers skill, confidence, and readiness only. No body talk, no timelines, no cure claims.
- Name Aurora in every issue.
- Nothing invented: no names, schools, times, results, or rewards without Ryne.
- The closing line signs off. The P.S. is the single ask and rotates with the calendar. Issue 1 carries the dryland P.S.
- Sign-off: Thanks, Ryne Gioviano, CSCS, Achieve Sports Performance
- Sources in small gray Helvetica under the signature.

### 2.2 Subject line and preview text
- Write the subject as a plain headline for a parent: name the topic and the athlete. Examples of the form (topics must match the real tip): "What your swimmer should drink on meet day" or "How to warm up between events." Avoid teasers the first line does not pay off.
- Always fill Mailchimp's preview text field. Use it to finish the subject's thought, for example "One thing to pack in the bag before Saturday." Never leave it blank, or inboxes show "Hi Name,".
- Write 5 or more subject options per issue and pick one. If Mailchimp A/B testing is used, judge the winner on clicks or replies, not opens (Litmus MPP data).

### 2.3 Body skeleton (about 150 to 250 words)
1. Hi *|FNAME|*,
2. Hook: one or two sentences that pick up the subject line. A real moment from the gym in Aurora, or the plain problem the parent sees.
3. The tip: what to do, when, and how much, in plain sentences.
4. Why it works: one or two sentences, tied to a cited source.
5. The athlete line: "Tell your athlete: ..." in one sentence.
6. Sign-off sentence (no ask), then: Thanks, Ryne Gioviano, CSCS, Achieve Sports Performance
7. P.S. (the only ask)
8. Sources, small gray Helvetica.

### 2.4 P.S. rotation
- Issue 1 (dryland, ahead of the November swimmer push starting Nov 14): P.S. High school swim season is wrapping up. Athletes who start dryland now get the most off-season weeks before club season. Reply "dryland" and we'll set up their evaluation.
- Referral P.S.: runs only once Ryne supplies the real reward. Draft: P.S. Know a teammate who'd want this plan? Forward this email. If they sign up, you both get [reward from Ryne].
- Future slots (each needs Ryne's real dates and details): tryout or season-start readiness, a named program block with its real start date.
- Every P.S. should give a true reason for now and a one-word reply.

### 2.5 Measuring an issue
- Primary: replies to the P.S. keyword and evaluations booked from them.
- Secondary: clicks and unsubscribes (Mailchimp all-user unsubscribe benchmark is 0.22%, Mailchimp page above).
- Ignore open rate as a success measure because of MPP (Litmus).

## Part 3. One-page checklist for each issue

**Before writing**
- [ ] One tip only, useful to a parent this month. Picked to fit the calendar.
- [ ] Source for the tip found and named (real, with link if possible).
- [ ] Any story, name, school, time, or result confirmed by Ryne. Otherwise leave it out.
- [ ] P.S. for this issue chosen from the rotation, with real dates and details.

**Subject and preview**
- [ ] Subject is a plain headline naming the topic for a parent. No teaser the body does not pay off.
- [ ] 5 or more subject options written, one picked.
- [ ] Preview text field filled and finishes the subject's thought.

**Body**
- [ ] Opens "Hi *|FNAME|*,"
- [ ] First sentence continues the subject line.
- [ ] Aurora named.
- [ ] Tip says what, when, and how much in full plain sentences.
- [ ] One "Tell your athlete" line.
- [ ] About 150 to 250 words. Readable by a skimmer from first and last lines.
- [ ] Closing sentence signs off with no ask.
- [ ] Signature exactly: Thanks, Ryne Gioviano, CSCS, Achieve Sports Performance

**P.S.**
- [ ] It is the only ask in the email.
- [ ] True reason for now plus a one-word reply.
- [ ] Intake called "20-minute screen" or "evaluation."

**House-rule sweep**
- [ ] No prices.
- [ ] No long-dash punctuation anywhere.
- [ ] No banned intake words and no banned hype words from the brand lock.
- [ ] Youth copy limited to skill, confidence, and readiness.
- [ ] Nothing invented. Every number has a named source.

**Format**
- [ ] Mailchimp, white background, Helvetica, one image at most.
- [ ] Sources in small gray Helvetica under the signature.
- [ ] Test send to a phone and to Gmail; check that the preview line reads well.

**After sending**
- [ ] Log replies, booked evaluations, clicks, and unsubscribes. Do not judge by opens.
